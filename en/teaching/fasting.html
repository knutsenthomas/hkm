Fasting – making more room for God

What is biblical fasting, why do we fast, and how can you get started safely? A teaching for you who want to join 21 days of prayer and fasting.

  • About 12 min read
  • 4 videos

The big idea

Fasting is setting something aside to make more room for God. It is not an achievement or a requirement, but an invitation to seek him – in secret, with a heart that turns to God and to our neighbour.

  1. 1Read13 short parts · about 12 min
  2. 2Watch4 videos from BibleProject
  3. 3ReflectAnswer questions and write

Fasting is one of the oldest and simplest ways to seek God. Yet many have never tried it, or have tried and given up because it felt like a demand they could not live up to. This teaching is written for you who want to understand what fasting is, why it is worth it, and how you can get started. It also works as preparation for 21 days of prayer and fasting.

What is fasting?

In the Bible, fasting means going without food for a period of time in order to seek God. The Hebrew word and the Greek both mean abstaining from food. But the point has never been the break from food itself. The point is to turn your whole self towards God.

When we fast, we say with our body what our heart is praying: “God, I need you more than I need the things I usually lean on.” Fasting is therefore not a diet, a hunger strike to pressure God, or an achievement that makes us more valuable. It is a way of clearing space so that we become more attentive to him.

Fasting is not the same as intermittent fasting. Today many people talk about fasting as a health or weight trend, for example 16:8 or 5:2. It may look similar from the outside, but the goal is completely different. Intermittent fasting is about the body. Christian fasting is about God. If we do not use the fast for prayer and for seeking him, it is just a break from food. It is not wrong to notice that the body benefits from a break, but that is not why we fast.

Many people today also fast from things other than food: social media, TV, streaming, shopping or other habits that fill our time and thoughts. That is not the same as biblical fasting from food, but the principle is the same. We set something aside to make more room for God.

Key point

Fasting is not an achievement, but a way of saying with your body: “God, I need you more than anything else.”

Fasting throughout the Bible

Fasting runs like a thread through all of Scripture.

Moses fasted for forty days on the mountain when he received the law (Exod 34:28). David fasted and prayed when his son was ill (2 Sam 12:16). Ezra’s people fasted and prayed for protection before the dangerous journey home to Jerusalem (Ezra 8:21–23). Esther asked all the Jewish people to fast for three days before she went in to the king at the risk of her life (Esth 4:16). Daniel fasted and prayed for his people, and later he kept a partial fast for three weeks in which he ate no rich food, meat or wine (Dan 9:3; Dan 10:2–3). The whole city of Nineveh fasted and repented when Jonah proclaimed judgement, and God showed mercy (Jonah 3:5–10).

In the New Testament Jesus fasted for forty days in the wilderness before he began his public ministry (Matt 4:1–2). The prophetess Anna served God in the temple “with fasting and prayer night and day” (Luke 2:37). The first church fasted when they sought God’s guidance. It was while they were fasting and praying that the Holy Spirit sent out Barnabas and Saul (Acts 13:2–3), and they fasted and prayed when they appointed elders in the churches (Acts 14:23).

The pattern is clear: God’s people fasted at decisive moments – when they needed guidance, when they were in distress, when they wanted to repent, and when they wanted to give themselves completely to God.

Fasting in church history

Fasting did not stop once the New Testament was finished. In the , one of the oldest Christian writings we have outside the Bible, believers are told to fast on Wednesdays and Fridays. Fasting was a regular part of everyday life for the first Christians, not something only for the especially spiritual.

Throughout church history, fasting has followed God’s people: in Lent before Easter, in revivals and in prayer movements. John Wesley, whose ministry gave rise to the Methodist church, fasted regularly on Wednesdays and Fridays himself and encouraged others to do the same. Many revivals have started with a few people beginning to fast and pray.

So when we fast today, we are not doing anything new or extreme. We are stepping into a habit that Christians all over the world have kept for two thousand years.

Jesus expected us to fast

In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus says: “When you fast …” (Matt 6:16), not “if you fast.” He takes it for granted that his disciples will fast, in the same way he takes it for granted that they will give and pray (Matt 6:2–6).

When someone asked why his disciples did not fast, Jesus answered that the friends of the bridegroom do not fast while the bridegroom is with them. “The time will come when the bridegroom will be taken from them; then they will fast” (Matt 9:15). We are living in those days now. Jesus has gone to the Father, and we long for him to come again. Fasting is part of that longing.

At the same time Jesus warns against fasting to be seen. The Pharisees turned fasting into a show and received their reward in the form of people’s admiration. Jesus says we should rather put oil on our head and wash our face, so that no one notices we are fasting, “and your Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you” (Matt 6:17–18). Fasting is first and foremost something between you and God.

Why do we fast?

There are many good reasons to fast. Here are some of the most important.

8:15 BibleProject · New TestamentMatthew 1–13 – overview

To draw closer to God

The most important reason is God himself. “Seek first his kingdom and his righteousness” (Matt 6:33). When your stomach growls, it becomes a reminder to turn to God. Many find that fasting sharpens their spiritual senses, makes it easier to hear God’s voice and gives a deeper hunger for the Word. Jesus himself said: “Man shall not live on bread alone, but on every word that comes from the mouth of God” (Matt 4:4).

For humility and repentance

In the Bible, fasting is often connected with humbling ourselves before God and turning away from sin. “Return to me with all your heart, with fasting and weeping and mourning” (Joel 2:12). Fasting reveals what really drives us. What we turn to for comfort comes into the light when we set it aside. That can be painful, but it is a gracious pain. God does not show us this to shame us, but to set us free.

For guidance and clarity

The first church fasted when they needed to know God’s will (Acts 13:2–3; 14:23). If you are facing an important decision, or feel you need direction for a new year, fasting is a good way to seek God. It is not about forcing an answer, but about being open and listening.

For breakthrough and freedom

Isaiah 58 describes the fast God delights in: “to loose the chains of injustice, untie the cords of the yoke, set the oppressed free” (Isa 58:6). In some manuscripts of Mark 9:29 Jesus says that certain things only come out “by prayer and fasting.” Fasting does not give us power over God, but it makes us more dependent on his power. Many have experienced strongholds broken, wounds healed and prayers answered in times of fasting.

For others

Fasting is not only for our own sake. Esther fasted for her whole people. Daniel fasted for the nation. Many fast and pray for family, church, city and country. Isaiah 58 also connects fasting with caring for others: sharing your bread with the hungry and welcoming those who have no home (Isa 58:7). The money and time we save by fasting can be given away.

The fast God delights in

Isaiah 58 is one of the most important chapters on fasting in the Bible. The people complained: “Why have we fasted, and you have not seen it?” God answered that they fasted, but at the same time exploited their workers, quarrelled and lived as before. Their fast was a ritual without a heart.

This is an important reminder. Fasting is not a religious achievement that makes us better than others. What counts is a heart that turns to God and to our neighbour. God promises that when fasting springs from such a heart, “your light will break forth like the dawn, and your healing will quickly appear” (Isa 58:8).

Different kinds of fasting

There is no single right way to fast. Here are some common forms:

  • Full fast: You eat no food but drink water. Jesus fasted like this in the wilderness. Longer periods of full fasting should only be done with good knowledge, preferably in consultation with a doctor.
  • Partial fast (Daniel fast): You eat simple food and abstain from something, for example meat, sweets, coffee or processed food, as Daniel did for three weeks (Dan 10:3). This is a good form for many who fast for 21 days.
  • Meal fast: You skip one or more meals a day and use the time for prayer and Bible reading.
  • Fasting from things other than food: You put away social media, screen time or other habits. This can be a good alternative if you cannot fast from food for health reasons.

The most important thing is not how much you abstain from, but that you use the fast to seek God.

Fasting with care for your body

Our body is a temple of the Holy Spirit (1 Cor 6:19), and God wants us to take care of it. Fasting should not harm you.

  • Talk to your doctor before fasting from food if you have diabetes, heart or kidney disease, take regular medication, are pregnant or breastfeeding, or have had a difficult relationship with food. Choose another form of fasting instead.
  • If you are older and have a chronic illness, are underweight, or do heavy physical work, you should also be careful. A partial fast or fasting from screens may be a better choice.
  • If you take medication, do not stop taking it or change the dose on your own. Some medicines, for example for diabetes, may need adjusting when you eat less, and your doctor should help you with that.
  • Children and young people should not fast from food over time. They can fast from screens or sweets instead.
  • Drink enough water, and consider cutting down on coffee and sugar a few days before you start, so you avoid headaches.
  • Start gently if you have never fasted before. Begin with one meal or one day.
  • End a longer fast gradually with light food.
  • If you become dizzy, unwell or have other symptoms, it is okay to eat. God is not disappointed in you.
Key point

Fasting should never harm you. Choose a form that fits your body and your life.

How to get started

1. Decide why. Write down what you want to seek God for. It might be closeness to him, guidance, a breakthrough, your family or something else on your heart.

2. Choose what and how long. Decide in advance what you will fast from and for how long. That makes it easier to stay with it when it gets hard.

3. Replace, don’t just remove. Use the time you would otherwise spend on food or screens for prayer, Bible reading and silence. A reading plan can be a great help.

4. Be prepared for resistance. The first days can be tough, both physically and spiritually. Irritation, hunger and doubt are common. See it as an invitation to pray, not as a sign that you have failed.

5. Fast together with others. It is easier to keep going when you are not alone. Invite a friend, your family or someone from church to join you.

6. Be gracious with yourself. If you stumble and eat what you had decided not to, simply start again. Fasting is about relationship, not about passing a test.

Questions and answers

Can I drink coffee or tea?

It depends on which fast you have chosen. On a full fast many drink only water. On a Daniel fast many choose to cut out coffee. The most important thing is that you decide in advance, and that your choice helps you seek God.

What do I do if I have to eat at work or with others?

You don’t need to explain yourself. Jesus said fasting should be done in secret. Choose simple food, or make a small exception for the sake of fellowship. God sees your heart.

Can I exercise while fasting?

A gentle walk and light activity are usually fine. Hard training can make you dizzy and weak, especially on a full fast. Listen to your body, and scale back during the fasting period.

What if I can’t manage the whole period?

Then you have still sought God on the days you fasted. Fasting is not a test you pass or fail. You can continue with a lighter form, or start again the next day.

Is it wrong to say that I am fasting?

No. Jesus warns against fasting to be seen, not against others knowing about it. When we fast together as a church, it is natural to talk about it and encourage one another.

What you can expect

Some experience great things when they fast: God speaking clearly, deep peace, answered prayer and breakthrough. Others mostly find it demanding, and that change happens quietly and gradually. Both are completely normal.

God sees your fast even when you don’t feel anything. Jesus promises that the Father, who sees what is done in secret, will reward you (Matt 6:18). Often it is only afterwards that we see what God did: that we became more sensitive to his voice, that a habit lost its grip, that a relationship was healed, or that we found new joy in prayer and Bible reading.
